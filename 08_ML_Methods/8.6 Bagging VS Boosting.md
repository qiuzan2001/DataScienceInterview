### Why “Bagging ↓ Variance” and “Boosting ↓ Bias”

<!-- priority-banner -->
> [!important] 优先级 **⭐⭐⭐ 必背** ｜ 重点：偏差方差・并行性・如何选
> 🚀 捷径：[[02. 三条学习路径]] ・ 📋 公式：[[04. 公式速查卡]] ・ ✅ 自测：[[99.5 Boosting 与集成]] ・ 🔎 追问：[[05. 高频追问 TOP 30]]


#### 1. Recap: What are **Bias** and **Variance**?

For any model $\hat f$ trained on data $\mathcal D$,

$$
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal D}\!\big[(\hat f(\mathbf x)-f(\mathbf x))^{2}\big]
=\underbrace{\big(\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal D}[\hat f(\mathbf x)]-f(\mathbf x)\big)^{2}}_{\textbf{Bias}^{2}}
+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal D}\!\big[(\hat f(\mathbf x)-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal D}[\hat f(\mathbf x)])^{2}\big]}_{\textbf{Variance}}
+\sigma_\text{noise}^{2}.
$$

* **Bias** = systematic error (model is “off-target”).
* **Variance** = sensitivity to the specific sample (model predictions “wiggle” if you retrain).

Good models keep **both** small, but different ensemble tricks attack them from different angles.

---

#### 2. **Bagging = Bootstrap Aggregating ⇒ Variance Killer**

1. **Process**

   * Draw $M$ bootstrap samples (same size as the training set, sampled *with* replacement).
   * Train an **unstable** base learner (e.g., a deep decision tree) on each sample.
   * Average their predictions (regression) or take a majority vote (classification).

2. **How it shrinks variance**

   *Picture  $M$ meteorologists independently forecasting tomorrow’s temperature; average them and the random day-to-day quirks cancel out.*

   * Each learner’s variance: $\sigma^{2}$.
   * If they were perfectly **independent**, the ensemble variance would be $\sigma^{2}/M$.
   * In reality the trees are correlated (they often see overlapping points), so it’s

     $$
     \text{Var}(\text{Bag}) = \rho\,\sigma^{2} + \frac{1-\rho}{M}\,\sigma^{2},
     $$

     where $\rho$ = average correlation between learners.
     More trees ⇒ second term $\downarrow$. Bootstrap + feature sub-sampling (Random Forests) drive $\rho$ $\downarrow$.

3. **Why bias barely budges**

   Every tree is grown *to exhaustion* (no pruning). A fully grown tree has very low bias—it already memorizes the training set—so the ensemble’s prediction mean is almost identical to any single tree’s mean. Averaging doesn’t nudge that mean toward the truth; it only damps the scatter around it.

4. **When bagging shines**

   * High-variance learners (deep trees, neural nets on smallish data).
   * Datasets with noisy fluctuations that we don’t want the final model to chase.

---

#### 3. **Boosting = Sequential Error-Correcting ⇒ Bias Buster**

1. **Process (AdaBoost / Gradient Boosting view)**

   * Start with a first weak learner $h_1$ (e.g., a shallow tree).
   * Look at its **residuals** (the mistakes), emphasize them, and train $h_2$ focused on what $h_1$ got wrong.
   * Repeat for $h_3, h_4,\dots,h_T$; final model is a *weighted* sum $F_T=\sum_{t=1}^T\alpha_t h_t$.

2. **How it trims bias**

   *Think of it as iterative proofreading: each round fixes leftover typos.*

   * Each stage adds a small vector in **function space** pointing toward the negative gradient of the loss ⇒ gradient descent toward the Bayes-optimal function.
   * Shallow trees (a few leaves) have **high bias** on their own, but each step chips away at the systematic error, so $\text{Bias}^2 \downarrow$ steeply with $T$.

3. **What happens to variance**

   *Every round adds parameters ⇒ more flexibility, hence potentially more wobble.* 更准确的说法是：**Boosting 主要降偏差；对方差的影响取决于正则化与早停**。

   * 学习率小（shrinkage）+ 树浅 + 早停（+ 子采样）时：每轮只加很小的修正，**方差通常可控，甚至会下降**——这也是现代 GBDT 的默认配方。
   * 轮数过多且没有正则化时：模型容量持续膨胀，开始拟合训练集的个体噪声 ⇒ **方差上升**，最终盖过偏差收益（经典过拟合曲线）。

   **Controls**: small learning rate (shrinkage), subsampling (stochastic GB), early stopping.

4. **When boosting shines**

   * Underfitting scenarios (simple models missing patterns).
   * Datasets where capturing subtle structure matters more than a bit of extra variance.
   * Competitions / tabular data where Gradient Boosting Trees dominate.

---

#### 4. Side-by-Side Intuition

| Property                | **Bagging**                                                 | **Boosting**                                       |
| ----------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| Base learner style      | **Complex, high-variance** (deep tree)                      | **Simple, high-bias** (stump/3-level tree)         |
| Learner training        | *Parallel*, independent                                     | *Sequential*, each learner sees weighted residuals |
| Main error term reduced | **Variance**: averaging random fluctuations                 | **Bias**: iterative residual correction            |
| Risk to watch           | Still biased if each tree biased; correlation between trees | Rising **variance** after many rounds **without regularization / early stopping** (over-fit) |
| Regularisation knobs    | # of trees, feature/subsample, max depth                    | Learning rate, # rounds, subsample, tree depth     |
| Analogies               | Poll-averaging, wisdom of crowds                            | Relay team of editors, gradient descent            |

---

#### 5. A Tiny Numerical Thought Experiment

Suppose one deep tree has:

*Bias* ≈ 0.2  *Variance* ≈ 1.0

---

**Bagging 100 trees**

* Bias stays ≈ 0.2.
* Variance ≈ $1.0/100 = 0.01$ (ignoring correlation).
* Total MSE ≈ $0.2^{2} + 0.01 = 0.05$.

---

**Boosting 100 shallow trees**

* Bias might drop to ≈ 0.05.
* Variance could rise to ≈ 0.4 (more complex overall).
* Total MSE ≈ $0.05^{2} + 0.4 = 0.4025$.

If we stop boosting at, say, 30 rounds: bias ≈ 0.07, variance ≈ 0.15 ⇒ MSE ≈ 0.1549 — better than either extreme.
*Lesson:* Bias–variance trade-off is always about the sweet spot; the two techniques just move the curve in different directions.

---

#### 6. Practical Rules of Thumb

1. **Diagnose first**
   *Cross-validated* error much higher than training error → high variance → consider Bagging / Random Forests.
   Training error high too → high bias → consider Boosting (plus deeper trees or richer features).

2. **Combine wisely**
   Random Forest → stable base; feed its *leaf-indices* as features into Gradient Boosting on top; or conversely use **bagged boosted trees** (add randomness to each boosting round). Modern libraries (e.g., XGBoost, LightGBM) already blend tricks from both worlds.

3. **Tune with a leash**

   * Bagging: more trees rarely hurts but mind runtime; control correlation via feature sampling.
   * Boosting: prefer low learning rate + many rounds **with early-stopping** instead of high learning rate.

---

### Take-Away Sentence

> **Bagging** washes out randomness by averaging many unstable models—great for taming **variance**—while **Boosting** marches in a straight line fixing residual errors—great for shrinking **bias** (but keep an eye on variance as you march). 🚀

## 🗣️ 英文表达 / How to Say It

**30-second version**

> "The one-line version is that bagging reduces variance and boosting reduces bias. Bagging trains independent deep trees on bootstrap samples and averages them in parallel; boosting trains shallow trees sequentially, each one on what the previous ensemble got wrong."

**Mechanism**

> "Each bagged tree is grown to exhaustion, so its bias is already low and averaging only damps the scatter — the error converges as the number of trees grows. Boosting starts from a high-bias weak learner, but each stage moves the function in the direction of the negative gradient, so the bias drops steeply with the number of rounds."

**If they push back**

> "A common pitfall is saying boosting reduces variance as well. What it does to variance depends on regularisation and early stopping: a small learning rate, shallow trees and early stopping usually keep variance controlled or even reduce it, but many unregularised rounds push it up. My rule of thumb for choosing: if cross-validated error is much worse than training error, that's variance — go bagging; if training error is high too, that's bias — go boosting."
